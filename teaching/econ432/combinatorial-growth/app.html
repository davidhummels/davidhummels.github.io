If ideas combine, why doesn't growth explode?

New technologies are combinations of old ones, so the number of possible new ideas grows explosively. Yet income per person in the US has grown a steady 2% a year for a century. Each tab lets you play with one answer to that puzzle: testing ideas costs resources, better ideas get harder to find, and our ability to search fades as knowledge grows. Every tab has an AI assist switch so you can ask whether AI changes the logic or just the numbers.

Start with a stock of ideas. Every period, each new idea can be paired with every idea that already exists, and a fixed share of those new pairs turn out to be useful. The useful ones join the stock and become ingredients for the next round. The math

possible pairs: P(N) = N(N−1)/2
new pairs this period: P(Nt) − P(Nt−1)
Nt+1 = Nt + r × [new pairs]

This is the version in your pre-work spreadsheet and in Clancy's essay. With 100 ideas there are 4,950 pairs; at r = 1%, 49 are useful. Now there are 149 ideas, 11,026 pairs, 6,076 of them new, so 61 more useful ideas. Each new idea pairs with everything already in the stock, so each round adds more than the last.

Why it can fizzle or explode

one new idea → about N new pairs → r × N useful ideas

Call r × N the idea's reproduction number, like the R of an epidemic. If each new idea produces less than one useful new idea (r × N < 1), the process dies out. If it produces more than one, each round is bigger than the last, and because N keeps growing, the reproduction number itself keeps rising. That's faster than exponential growth. Try 50 ideas at 1%, then 2%, then 100 ideas at 1%.

Weitzman's brake

new ideas per year ≈ (s × GDP ÷ cost per test) × r

Someone has to test each candidate. Once the candidates outrun what the budget can test, ideas grow only as fast as the budget, which grows with GDP. Turn on the budget limit and watch the blue line bend toward steady growth. Then turn on AI. Cheaper tests shift the line up a lot. Do they change its long-run slope?

Weitzman, M. (1998), "Recombinant Growth," Quarterly Journal of Economics 113(2). Clancy, M., "Combinatorial Innovation and Technological Progress in the Very Long Run," New Things Under the Sun. If any subset of ideas can combine (not just pairs), Arthur (2009) counts 2N − N − 1 possibilities, which explodes even faster.

A new combination has to do more than work. It has to beat the best idea we already have. If the quality of a random try follows a bell curve, the chance of beating the current best is the area of the curve to the right of it, and that area shrinks very fast as the best gets better. On the right, watch a research program climb: each year it makes a number of tries and keeps the best one it has ever found.

The math

P(a try beats best q*) = 1 − Φ(q*)
expected tries to improve = 1 ÷ [1 − Φ(q*)]

Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. A best one standard deviation above average is beaten by about 16% of tries (1 in 6). Three: 0.13% (1 in 741). Five: one try in 3.5 million. The tail thins so fast that each step up costs far more than the last.

Contrast with the quality ladder

In lecture 20's quality ladder, each rung was better by the same factor λ and cost the same research effort no matter how high you'd climbed, so steady research gave steady growth. Here the rungs get harder. With a fixed number of tries per year, progress slows to a crawl. To keep climbing at a steady pace, tries have to grow, which is what exploding combinations (tab 1) and rising R&D spending supply. Jones (2021) shows the two forces can offset each other almost exactly.

Re-domaining: a new bell curve

Stretching an old principle means drawing deeper into its thin tail. A new principle (Arthur's re-domaining) is a fresh distribution. Its average try may be worse than today's best, but with a wider spread a much larger share of its tries beat the best. Try switching once, then let the program switch automatically whenever progress stalls. Growth becomes a series of new principles.

Jones, C. (2021), "Recipes and Economic Growth: A Combinatorial March Down an Exponential Tail," NBER WP 28340. Arthur, W. B. (2009), The Nature of Technology, chapters 6–7. Quality is drawn from a normal distribution; the best of n tries is drawn exactly from the distribution of the maximum.

You can only combine ideas you know. As the stock of knowledge grows, each person knows a smaller share of it. Here the useful new ideas come only from pairs someone can actually see. The key setting is how fast what one person knows keeps up with everything that's known.

AI assist

Agrawal, McHale and Oettl think AI's biggest contribution is retrieval: helping people find and use what's already known outside their field.

The math

what a person knows: K = N01−φ · Nφ
pairs a team of m can see: V = P(min(N, m·K))
Nt+1 = Nt + hm × Vt

Each period, researchers find h useful ideas per pair they can see. At the start everyone knows everything (K = N0). If φ = 1, personal knowledge keeps up with the stock: everyone can see every pair, as in tab 1. If φ < 1, each person's share of what's known shrinks as knowledge grows. Teams pay a coordination cost: hm = h ÷ (1 + cost × (m − 1)).

The knife edge at φ = ½

growth rate = h × V ÷ N ≈ h × K² ÷ 2N ∝ N2φ−1

If φ = ½, K² grows exactly as fast as N, so the growth rate is constant: steady exponential growth. Above ½ the growth rate rises with N and growth explodes, as in tab 1. Below ½ it falls toward zero. Steady growth is what you get when what one person knows grows with the square root of everything known.

Teams and AI: level or slope?

Teams and an AI that multiplies everyone's reach both scale up what each searcher can see, which raises the growth rate by a constant factor but doesn't change whether it rises or falls. An AI that lets personal knowledge keep pace with the stock (a higher φ) changes the exponent, and with it the shape of growth. Which kind of tool do you think today's AI is?

Agrawal, A., J. McHale and A. Oettl (2019), "Finding Needles in Haystacks: Artificial Intelligence and Recombinant Growth," in The Economics of Artificial Intelligence. The model here is a simplified teaching version of their idea, built on the same pairs arithmetic as tab 1.
