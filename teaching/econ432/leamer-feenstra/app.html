Is it a demand curve or a supply curve?

Price and quantity data alone cannot tell you whether you are looking at a demand curve or a supply curve. The first tab builds up Leamer's bounds and Feenstra's identification. The next three show how Anson Soderbery's papers refine that estimator and test its assumptions.

Two markets share the same demand and supply slopes but differ in which curve gets hit hardest. In each market the (q, p) equilibria fill in, the direct and reverse regressions bracket a slope, and Leamer's hyperbola marks every (demand slope, supply slope) pair consistent with the data. Feenstra's step is to intersect the two hyperbolas inside the feasible quadrant.

The model

demand: pt = d·qt + ut, d < 0
supply: pt = s·qt + vt, s > 0

u and v are uncorrelated demand and supply shocks. Read p and q as log changes and the slopes are inverse elasticities: d = −1/σ for demand and s = ω for inverse supply in Feenstra's notation. Each period the market clears where the shifted curves cross.

Leamer (1981): one market, a hyperbola

E[(p − d·q)(p − s·q)] = 0
⇔ Vpp − (d+s)·Vpq + d·s·Vqq = 0

One equation in two unknowns traces a hyperbola in (d, s). Its asymptotes sit at the OLS slope Vpq/Vqq and it crosses the axes at the reverse-regression slope Vpp/Vpq. Imposing d < 0 < s bounds one slope between the two regressions and leaves the other unbounded. If Cov(p,q) > 0, supply is the bounded one.

Feenstra (1994): two markets, one point

Vpq,i·(d+s) − Vqq,i·(d·s) = Vpp,i, i = 1, 2

The condition is linear in the sum d+s and the product d·s, so two markets with different variance ratios pin both down. d and s are then the roots of t² − (d+s)t + ds = 0. The hyperbolas meet twice, as mirror images across the 45° line, and the sign restrictions pick one. With many supplying countries Feenstra stacks these conditions and estimates them as a cross-country regression. If σd/σs is the same in both markets, the hyperbolas coincide and nothing is identified.

Soderbery (2015), “Estimating import supply and demand elasticities: Analysis and implications,” Journal of International Economics 96.

The paper does both things. First it works back from Leamer to show what Feenstra's estimator actually does. It is 2SLS with exporter dummies as instruments. The first stage collapses each exporter's panel into its Leamer hyperbola, one point per exporter, and the second stage fits a line through those points. Feenstra (1994) noted the 2SLS equivalence; Soderbery uses it to diagnose the estimator. Second, it shows that in the short panels typical of trade data those instruments are weak: many hyperbolae are imprecise, 2SLS weights them too heavily, and its first step often lands outside the feasible region, where Broda and Weinstein's grid search takes over. The fix is LIML, which downweights imprecise hyperbolae, plus a constrained nonlinear LIML search in place of the grid.

The simulation follows the paper's Monte Carlo design: 55 exporters, σ = 3, ω = 0.5, shock variances drawn from U(0, 9), and data differenced against a reference exporter. It leaves out the paper's unbalanced panels and unit-value measurement error, so the size of the 2SLS bias here differs from the paper's. What carries over is that LIML stays closer to the truth in short panels.

Step 1 of 2SLS: from a panel to one point per exporter

what the exporter dummies do

Feenstra's regression has one observation per exporter and period: (Δp)² on (Δs)² and Δs·Δp. Those regressors are built from the same shocks as the error term, so they are endogenous. The instruments are a full set of exporter dummies. Regressing any variable on exporter dummies replaces each period's value with that exporter's own average over time. So the first stage turns T periods into three numbers per exporter: its Var(y), Var(x) and Cov(x, y). Those three numbers are exactly what define the exporter's Leamer hyperbola, which is why the panels below can draw each exporter as one curve or one point. The second stage is then a regression across exporters.

one period (▸ beyond the axis)exporter's average = first-stage fitted value

Why short panels are a problem: when T is small, the spread within each row is large compared with the differences between rows. Each exporter's averages are then noisy, the first stage is weak, and 2SLS treats every exporter's averages as if they were equally reliable. LIML also uses the spread within each row, so it can tell a precisely measured hyperbola from a noisy one. Drag T down to 5 and up to 45 to see the rows tighten.

Divide Feenstra's regression by the price–share covariance and it becomes br = θ1·(1/b) + θ2, where b is the OLS slope and br the reverse-regression slope for one exporter, θ1 = −d·s and θ2 = d + s. 2SLS is weighted least squares through these points with weights T·Cov(x, y)², so exporters with a large covariance pull the line hardest.

Soderbery (2018), “Trade elasticities, heterogeneity, and optimal tariffs,” Journal of International Economics 114.

Feenstra needs every exporter to share one supply elasticity. If each exporter j has its own ωj, each exporter's hyperbola passes through a different point, and intersecting them across exporters recovers nothing meaningful. Soderbery adds a second view of the same trade flow: the exporter's side, how its sales are split across destinations. That gives each exporter a second hyperbola through the same (σ, ωj). All exporters to one importer face the same σ, so the crossings line up on one vertical line, and each exporter's ωj is read off where its own pair crosses.

Simplified: here the export-side hyperbola is a second Leamer market with the same σ and ωj but its own shock variances. In the paper the export-side equation also involves the reference destination's elasticities, and both equations are estimated together by constrained nonlinear SUR. Here σ̂ is chosen to minimize the distance between each exporter's two hyperbolae, summed over exporters, and ω̂j is read off at σ̂. In finite samples each ω̂j is noisy, much more so than σ̂, which pools all exporters. More periods, or the box that forces sample Cov(u, v) = 0, shows the population picture.

Feenstra's estimator is an IV regression with exporter dummies as instruments, so two standard IV diagnostics apply. The first-stage F-statistic asks whether the hyperbolae differ enough from each other to pin down a crossing. If every exporter has the same mix of supply and demand shocks, the hyperbolae pile on top of each other and the instruments are weak. The Sargan J-test asks whether the hyperbolae share a common crossing, as they must when supply and demand shocks are uncorrelated within each exporter.

σ = 2.5 and ω = 0.5, as in the paper's Monte Carlos. Shock variances are log-normal across exporters with the spread you set. An F pass means the Cragg–Donald F is at or above your cutoff (the paper uses the Stock–Yogo critical value for LIML). A J pass means p ≥ 0.20, the paper's 80% rule. The J here is the textbook Sargan statistic, and the estimator is Fuller LIML. The paper uses a heteroskedasticity-robust LIML.

Things to try

  • Drag the variance spread toward zero. The hyperbolae bunch together, F falls below the cutoff, and new draws scatter the estimate.
  • Raise the spread of the correlation across exporters. The hyperbolae stop sharing a crossing and J rejects.
  • Raise the average correlation with zero spread. σ̂ is biased, but J mostly passes: the test sees violations that differ across exporters, not a correlation they all share.
